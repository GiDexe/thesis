\begin{center}
    {\Large\bfseries Abstract}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.8cm}

\noindent Voluntary standards are adopted internationally, but the structure of influence along which they travel is rarely observed and usually assumed. I analyse whether technical and political standards diffuse through different channels, without specifying those channels in advance. Using annual ISO 9001 (Quality) and ISO 14001 (Environment) certification counts for the 27 EU member states, I recover a separate latent diffusion network for each standard with the adaptive elastic-net GMM estimator of \citet{depaula2025}, inferring who influences whom from panel covariation in adoption alone. The two recovered networks are nearly disjoint and neither resembles geography, trade, or cultural proximity. Dyadic regressions sharpen the contrast: Quality links track bilateral FDI, locating the ``economic channel'' in investment; Environment links form between countries with asymmetric environmental-policy stances, consistent with a governance-based logic. Holding the recovered network fixed, the second-stage peer effect is positive and significant for Quality on the best-fitting network, but small and imprecise for Environment.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Policies are not adopted in a vacuum: they travel along structures of influence between
countries that researchers must usually assume rather than observe. \citet{gilardi2019},
in their review, report how the field is organised around four mechanisms---learning,
competition, coercion and emulation---while noting that the channel along which a policy
actually travels is rarely visible in the data. This logic is not confined to public
regulation. It extends to private and voluntary standards, of which International
Organisation for Standardisation (ISO) management-system certifications are the leading
case: sets of audited practices that began spreading internationally in the 1990s and now
constitute a large and growing catalogue.\footnote{Certification is verified by accredited
    bodies and coordinated internationally, first through the International Accreditation
    Forum (IAF) and, from January 2026, through its successor, Global Accreditation
    Cooperation Incorporated (Global ACI).} But not all policies are alike. The logic of
diffusion plausibly depends on what a standard is \emph{for}: a technical standard such
as a quality-management certification is adopted for operational, efficiency-driven reasons
and might therefore travel along production and supply-chain links; a political standard,
whose adoption can function as a signal to external audiences, is likely not to travel along
the same channels at all. Comparing a technical and a political standard on the same node
set is thus informative in a way that studying either in isolation is not.

Establishing these channels matters on two fronts. Substantively, the consequences of
policies that spread internationally can be first-order: \citet{linsenmeier2023} find that
carbon-pricing adoption in one country significantly raises the probability of subsequent
adoption in others, and that the emission reductions attributable to this international
spread can exceed those of any single country's own adoption---so knowing which channel
diffusion travels allows one to target effort where it will not arrive on its own.
Methodologically, relying on the wrong network biases the very quantities the exercise
seeks to measure---peer-effect strength and the speed of diffusion \citep{depaula2025,
    bramoulle2009}. In current literature, the diffusion network is almost always \emph{assumed} rather than
\emph{inferred}, however, recent advances in
network econometrics instead allow the diffusion-supporting network to be directly inferred from
panel covariation in adoption alone.

This paper asks whether technical and political standards diffuse through different
international channels, and answers it without assuming those channels in advance. I
focus on the most widely held standard of each type in the European Union: ISO~9001
(Quality Management Systems), the technical case, and ISO~14001 (Environmental Management Systems), the
political case. Adoption of Environment has been shown to operate partly as a
signalling device toward international audiences, being more prevalent in weak-governance
environments \citep{berliner2013}. Using annual certification counts for the 27 EU member
states (1993--2017 for Quality, 1997--2017 for Environment), I recover a separate latent
diffusion network for each standard with the adaptive elastic-net GMM estimator of
\citet{depaula2025}, which identifies who influences whom directly from the panel
covariation in adoption. I then compare
the two recovered structures, examine the bilateral determinants of the recovered links in
dyadic regressions, and re-estimate peer effects \citep{bramoulle2009} on both the
recovered networks and the exogenous benchmark networks of \citet{albuquerque2007}.

I find that the two standards do diffuse differently and along structures that no ex-ante
metric captures. The two recovered networks are nearly disjoint, and neither resembles exogenous benchmarks current literature accounts for \citet{albuquerque2007}. The dyadic regressions sharpen the contrast into a two-track reading: for
Quality, bilateral FDI predicts the weight of a recovered link while bilateral trade does
not, refining accounts that treat trade and investment jointly as the ``economic channel'';
for Environment, neither economic variable matters, and link weights are instead driven
between countries with asymmetric environmental-policy stances, consistent with a
governance-based rather than market-based logic. In the second stage, peer effects on the
recovered network are positive, significant and of plausible magnitude for Quality, while the exogenous proxies yield far larger and less precise
estimates, consistent with network misspecification inflating the peer-effect coefficient.
For Environment the peer effect is small and insignificant; reasons are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

Closest to this paper is \citet{albuquerque2007}, who compare the diffusion of the same
two standards by estimating a Bass diffusion model on a set of exogenous candidate
networks constructed from geography, trade and cultural similarity, arguing Quality is
spreading mainly through geography and trade, whereas Environment diffuses through
geography and culture. I extend their work by recovering the network endogenously, thus
removing the misspecification at the root of that bias, letting me quantify how much
substantive conclusions depend on the specification of the underlying network.

This paper speaks to three literatures. To the \emph{policy diffusion} literature
\citep{gilardi2019, linsenmeier2023}, it brings the network-recovery approach that
\citet{depaula2025} developed and applied to tax competition. It shows that in this
domain, too, the structure of influence along which adoption travels is not reducible to
the standard proximity proxies. To the literature on \emph{voluntary corporate standards}
\citep{guler2002, albuquerque2007, corbett2006, berliner2013, vanderheijden2020}, it contributes the first
network-recovery comparison of a technical and a political standard, and a decomposition
of the ``economic channel'' showing that, for Quality, the binding margin is investment
rather than trade. To the \emph{network econometrics} literature on peer effects
\citep{manski1993, bramoulle2009, depaula2025}, it provides an application that recovers
and contrasts two networks on the same node set.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:data-and-methodology}
presents the data and empirical methodology, including the network-recovery framework
and the econometric specification. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the main results,
starting with the properties of the recovered networks and their comparison with
exogenous benchmarks, followed by dyadic determinants and peer-effect estimates.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the main findings and limitations. The Appendix
provides additional robustness checks, including the grid search, degree distributions,
network similarity measures, and supplementary estimation results.

\section{Data and Methodology}
\label{sec:data-and-methodology}

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}
The standards diffusion is measured by the annual count of valid certificates to each standard, taken
from the ISO Survey of Management System Standard Certifications \citep{ISOsurvey}, the survey ISO conducts
each year at the national level worldwide. An observation is a
country--year count of valid certificates, where a certificate is the document issued by an
accredited certification body once an organisation has demonstrated conformity to the
standard. \footnote{The ISO Survey reports certificates rather than certified organisations
    or production sites; prior to 2017 a supplementary count of ``sites'' was also collected but
    was incomplete and was discontinued thereafter. The data providers are certification bodies
    accredited by members of the International Accreditation Forum, who participate on a
    voluntary basis, so country-level totals can fluctuate with participation. From 2018 the
    survey methodology changed in a way that breaks comparability with earlier years; the present
    sample therefore ends in 2017 and no post-2018 harmonisation is attempted.} The series begin
in the years the two standards were established, $1993$ for Quality (ISO~9001) and $1997$ for
Environment (ISO~14001), and both run through $2017$. The sample is restricted to the 27 EU member states, which cannot be enlarged much further without compromising network recovery at the panel length available here.\footnote{The methodology of \citet{depaula2025} recovers the interaction matrix $\hat{W}$ from moment conditions whose parameter count grows with $N^2$, while the identifying variation grows only with $N \times T$; enlarging the node set beyond what the panel length can support therefore makes estimation infeasible.} The EU-27, observed over $T=21$ (Environment), $T = 25$ (Quality), sits at the boundary of what the estimator can handle while retaining an institutionally coherent group: a common market with a shared conformity-assessment and accreditation infrastructure, which makes treating it as an approximately closed system defensible. The cost of this restriction  is essentially the truncation of influence arriving from outside the EU and is discussed in Section \ref{sec:limitations}.

This yields a balanced country dimension of $N=27$ for both standards resulting in $N\times T = 675$ and $567$ country--year observations for Quality and Environment respectively.

Network estimation covariates are drawn from the World Bank World Development Indicators \citep{WDI} and the
Worldwide Governance Indicators \citep{WGI}. From the WDI I take GDP per capita, Trade (\% of GDP),
the share of renewables in total final energy consumption, and total natural resource rents
(\% of GDP); from the WGI I take the Rule of Law estimate.
Dyadic regression covariates used in Section~\ref{sec:dyadic} are drawn from additional sources:
bilateral FDI positions from Eurostat's Balance of Payments statistics \citep{EurostatBOP};
bilateral trade flows from the CEPII BACI database \citep{BACI}; environmental tax revenue
per worker from Eurostat's environmental tax accounts \citep{EurostatENV}, divided by total
labour force from the WDI; geographic distance computed from capital-city coordinates via
the haversine formula \citep{mapsR}; and cultural distance as the Euclidean distance over
Hofstede's cultural dimensions \citep{hofstede2001}.

The structure of the data imposes the constraint of stopping the sample in 2017. The choice was necessary because of a change in the survey methodology used to collect the data, however it comes with the cost of reduced estimation precision for both standards. \citet{depaula2025} run Monte Carlo simulations on several network types (their Online Appendix Figure A1, p.~21), and the Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi random network provides a useful benchmark: in their analysis, at the $T$ range of my panels one can still expect some deviation from the "true" $W$ structure along with some downward bias in the estimates of the peer-effect parameter $\hat{\rho}$ and the exogenous social effect $\hat{\gamma}$. This cost is potentially more critical for Environment given its shorter panel ($T = 21$ versus $T = 25$).
\subsection{Descriptive Statistics}
The two standards show different adoption levels, consistently with their distinct institutional histories. As reported in Table~\ref{tab:desc_stats}, the older standard (Quality) is considerably more widespread than Environment, both in absolute and relative terms. In 2017, the total number of Quality certifications reached 315,987, compared with 84,316 Environment certifications. Similarly, certification intensity amounted to 441.67 certifications per thousand dollars of GDP per capita for Quality, against 128.86 for Environment. These differences are likely related to the earlier introduction of the Quality standard, which had already achieved widespread diffusion by the time Environment was launched in 1997.

To gauge the EU's place in global diffusion, the 27 member states accounted for 27.8\% of the 1{,}137{,}167 Quality certificates and 24.9\% of the 338{,}995 Environment certificates issued worldwide in 2017, a share that peaked near one third around 2010 (34.2\% and 31.9\%) and eased thereafter as adoption broadened outside Europe. This is a substantial concentration.

Despite these differences in adoption levels, the two standards may exhibit distinct diffusion dynamics. As argued by \citet{albuquerque2007}, the diffusion of the Environment standard may have benefited from the organisational capabilities and certification experience accumulated through the earlier adoption of the Quality standard. In this view, the later standard could spread more rapidly despite its lower overall prevalence.

\subsection{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\citet{depaula2025} extend the peer-effects literature \citep{manski1993, bramoulle2009} to the case in which the network of interactions is unknown. Stacking the $N$ countries observed in year $t$, their structural model is
\begin{equation}
    y_t = \rho_0 W_0 y_t + X_t \beta_0 + W_0 X_t \gamma_0 + \alpha_i + \alpha_t + \varepsilon_t,
    \label{eq:structural}
\end{equation}

which rearranges into the reduced form
\begin{equation}
    y_t = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \Pi_{0,k}\, x_{k,t} + \nu_t, \qquad
    \Pi_{0,k} = (I - \rho_0 W_0)^{-1}\!\left(\beta_{0,k} I + \gamma_{0,k} W_0\right).
    \label{eq:reduced}
\end{equation}
Here $y_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$ is the vector of outcomes across the $N$ countries in year $t$, $x_{k,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$ is the $k$-th column of the $N \times K$ covariate matrix $X_t$, $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is the interaction matrix, $\rho_0$ is the scalar endogenous peer effect, and $\beta_{0,k}, \gamma_{0,k}$ are the $k$-th elements of the direct and exogenous (contextual) effect vectors $\beta_0, \gamma_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{K}$, so that each multiplier $\Pi_{0,k}$ is $N \times N$.\footnote{The compact single-multiplier form $\Pi_0 = (I-\rho_0 W_0)^{-1}(\beta_0 I + \gamma_0 W_0)$ holds for a scalar covariate; with $K$ covariates the reduced form generalises element by element as above, following \citet[Appendix B]{depaula2025}.}

If $W_0$ were known, equation~\eqref{eq:reduced} would directly identify the structural parameters $(\rho_0,\beta_0,\gamma_0)$ from a reduced-form regression of outcomes on covariates. This is essentially the approach of \citet{bramoulle2009}, who show how it is possible to recover peer effects from a social network using the \citet{manski1993} linear in means model. The contribution of \citet{depaula2025} is to show that, when $W_0$ is unknown, $\Pi_0$ still contains enough information to recover both the network and the parameters jointly. The intuition is the one that underlies any diffusion process. If a contagious disease spreads through a population, the people who fall ill at the same time tend to be the ones who interact: knowing who got sick when, and what their characteristics are, one can work backwards to who must have been in contact with whom. The same logic applies here. If four countries adopt a standard in synchronous patterns that cannot be explained by their own characteristics alone, the synchrony itself is evidence of a link between them. Cross-country covariation in $y$, conditional on $x$, identifies who is connected to whom, and the strength of the covariation, suitably disentangled, identifies how strong the link is. In principle, $\Pi_0$ is identified as the linear projection of $y$ on $x$ and could be recovered by ordinary least squares whenever the panel is long enough relative to the number of parameters. In typical short panels this is infeasible. \citet{depaula2025} recover the network $\hat W$ and the structural parameters $(\hat\rho, \hat\beta, \hat\gamma)$ via an adaptive elastic-net GMM (\citet{caner2014}) defined directly on the moment conditions implied by the structural model. Sparsity in $\hat W$ regularises the high-dimensional problem and restores consistency in short panels, estimating the network from observed spillovers without requiring a priori commitment to any specific weighting scheme.

It is worth noting that their estimator imposes row-sum normalisation on $\hat{W}$ as part of the recovery with  network estimation and peer-effect identification occurring jointly. In my setting, where the recovered structure is sparse, this row-sum normalisation produces a nearly binarised network (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

My work applies this framework to recover the latent network underlying ISO certification diffusion across the $N=27$ EU member states, estimating it separately for the two standards. For each standard I obtain a distinct recovered network $\hat W$ together with its structural parameters $(\hat\rho,\hat\beta,\hat\gamma)$.

I specify the outcome and controls as follows. Following \citet{depaula2025}, the outcome is specified in first differences; given that annual certificate counts are highly heterogeneous and contain zeros for country--years preceding adoption (Table~\ref{tab:desc_stats}), I work with log certificates throughout. The estimated specification is equation~\ref{eq:structural}, where the outcome is the year-on-year log-difference in certificates, $y_{it} = \log(1+n_{it}) - \log(1+n_{i,t-1})$, with $n_{it}$ the number of valid certificates held in country $i$ in year $t$. The $K=5$ covariates enter as $\log(1+x)$: GDP per capita, Trade (\% of GDP), the Rule of Law estimate, Total Natural Resource Rents (\% of GDP), and the Share of Renewables in Total Final Energy Consumption.

I estimate equation~\eqref{eq:structural} via the adaptive elastic-net GMM, which operates with three penalty parameters ($\lambda_1$, $\lambda_2$, $\lambda_3$) regulating the strength of the combined RIDGE-LASSO, following \citet{depaula2025} penalties are selected via BIC over a grid search (Appendix~\ref{tab:tab:grid_search}).
I then analyse the structure of the two recovered networks and compare their similarity, both with one another and with the exogenous network specifications adopted by \citet{albuquerque2007}. I complement this with a dyadic regression of the recovered link weights on a set of bilateral covariates,
\begin{equation}
    \hat w_{ij} = \sum_{k} \delta_k\, D_{ij}^{k} + a_i + a_j + u_{ij},
    \label{eq:dyadic}
\end{equation}
where $D_{ij}^{k}$ collects the dyadic regressors (economic distance, asymmetry of environmental policy, cultural distance, geographic distance, bilateral FDI, and bilateral trade), and $a_i$ and $a_j$ are sender and receiver fixed effects absorbing each country's overall propensity to influence and to be influenced \citet{dzemski2019}. The regressors follow the existing literature. Bilateral FDI, bilateral trade and geographic distance enter as $\log(1+x)$; economic distance and environmental-policy asymmetry are absolute gaps in logged variables, $|\log x_i - \log x_j|$, for GDP per capita and environmental tax per worker respectively; cultural distance is the Euclidean distance over Hofstede cultural dimensions (2015 Hofstede Insights release), in levels.\footnote{The Hofstede framework scores national cultures along a set of dimensions (e.g.\ power distance, individualism, uncertainty avoidance); see \citet{hofstede2001}. Cultural distance between two countries is the Euclidean distance between their score vectors, so that larger values denote more dissimilar national cultures.}
Having recovered both the network and the associated structural parameters, I assess the role of the network in a second stage. For comparability with the exogenous benchmarks of \citet{albuquerque2007}, I take $\hat W$ as given and estimate the peer effect with a strategy common to all specifications. Following \citet{bramoulle2009}, I estimate the linear-in-means peer-effects model
\begin{equation}
    y_{it} = \rho \sum_{j} \hat W_{ij}\, y_{jt} + \beta\, x_{it} + \gamma \sum_{j} \hat W_{ij}\, x_{jt} + \alpha_i + \alpha_t + \varepsilon_{it},
    \label{eq:bdf}
\end{equation}
where the endogenous peer term $\sum_j \hat W_{ij} y_{jt}$ is instrumented by the characteristics of indirectly connected peers, $\mathbf{\hat W}^2\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{\hat W}^3\mathbf{x}$, which exploit the intransitivities of the network to address the reflection problem \citep{manski1993,bramoulle2009}. I estimate equation~\eqref{eq:bdf} on my recovered network and my exogenous benchmarks of \citet{albuquerque2007} (both row-normalised), and including country and year fixed effects. The recovered network is expected to be the best estimate of the true network of interactions; as validation, I compare the within-$R^2$ across specifications.

As a robustness check, I also estimate a lagged specification in which all explanatory variables are dated $t-1$:
\begin{equation}
    y_{it}
    =
    \theta y_{i,t-1}
    +
    \rho_1 \sum_j \hat W_{ij} y_{j,t-1}
    +
    \sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{1k} x_{i,t-1}^{(k)}
    +
    \sum_{k=1}^{K}\delta_{1k}\sum_j \hat W_{ij}x_{j,t-1}^{(k)}
    +
    \alpha_i+\alpha_t+\varepsilon_{it},
    \label{eq:fullag}
\end{equation}
where $\hat W$ denotes either the recovered network or one of the benchmark exogenous networks.

This specification can be viewed as a restricted version of the dynamic framework discussed by \citet{bramoulle2020}, in which contemporaneous peer effects are excluded and social influence is allowed to propagate only with a one-period delay. By replacing the contemporaneous peer outcome $Wy_t$ with its lagged counterpart $Wy_{t-1}$, the model breaks the simultaneous feedback mechanism that gives rise to the reflection problem \citep{manski1993, bramoulle2009}. Identification therefore relies on temporal rather than contemporaneous spillovers. This break is only partial, however: the peer term is freed of contemporaneous feedback, but $\hat W$ is itself recovered from contemporaneous covariation (eq.~\ref{eq:structural}), so the lagged specification does not fully remove the contemporaneous information embedded in the network.

The parameter $\rho_1$ measures the effect of past peer adoption on current adoption. Because influence is constrained to operate through lagged outcomes and characteristics, this specification provides a conservative robustness check on the contemporaneous estimates, reducing the risk that recovered network effects merely reflect common shocks affecting EU countries simultaneously.

\subsection{Network Recovery}
\label{sec:network-recovery}
\input{assets/tables/network_stats.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:network_eu} reports the structural summary of the two networks selected by BIC. Three features stand out. First, both recovered networks are \emph{sparse}: the Quality network retains $28$ directed links and the Environment network $30$. Their density---the share of all possible directed links that are realised---is therefore similar, $0.040$ for Quality and $0.043$ for Environment. This is the regularisation at work: the adaptive elastic-net penalty drives the overwhelming majority of off-diagonal entries of $\hat W$ to zero, which is precisely the property that restores consistency in a short panel. Second, the two structures are \emph{nearly disjoint}: of their respective $28$ and $30$ links, only $3$ coincide in both endpoints and direction, leaving $25$ links unique to Quality and $27$ unique to Environment. This near-orthogonality is the central descriptive finding of the paper and is quantified formally by the Jaccard index in Section~\ref{sec:network-similarity}. Third, the two networks differ in internal topology. The Quality network is more clustered (coefficient $0.164$ versus $0.053$) and exhibits some mutual links ($7.1\%$ of links are reciprocated), whereas the Environment network has \emph{no} reciprocated links at all and a markedly lower clustering coefficient, pointing to a more hierarchical, less locally cohesive diffusion structure. The recovered peer-effect parameter $\hat\rho$ is 0.203 and 0.286 for the Quality and Environment standards, respectively. Neither is individually significant, nor are the covariate coefficients (Figure~\ref{fig:gmm_ci}). Two considerations follow. First, \citet{depaula2025} document in their Monte Carlo simulations that $\hat\rho$ is underestimated at short panel lengths, with the bias vanishing only as $T$ grows; at the $T$ available to me the recovery-stage estimate is therefore expected to be both downward-biased and imprecise. Second, this $\hat\rho$ is not the object I wish to compare across networks: it is estimated jointly with $\hat{W}$ and is specific to the recovered structure, so it cannot be set against the exogenous benchmarks on a common footing. Both considerations motivate the second-stage analysis of Section~\ref{sec:peer-effects}, where I take $\hat{W}$ as given and re-estimate the peer effect on the row-nomalised networks following \citet{bramoulle2009}.


Figure~\ref{fig:networks} renders the two recovered networks and makes these features visible. The panels are arranged as a $2\times 2$ grid: the diagonal shows each network in its own optimal layout, while the off-diagonal panels redraw each network using the other standard's coordinates, so that a shared structure, if present, would appear as aligned links across a row or column. However, neither network retains any recognisable backbone when projected onto the other's coordinates, confirming that the dissimilarity documented in Table~\ref{tab:network_eu} is not an artefact of layout.

As anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, the sparsity of the recovered networks has a direct consequence for the weights: with most nodes receiving only one or two incoming links (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:degree_hist}), row normalisation mechanically pushes the surviving weights toward one, so that the recovered network is nearly binarised. What matters is then which links exist. The diagonal panels of Figure~\ref{fig:networks} reveal how the topological contrast of Table~\ref{tab:network_eu} manifests structurally. The Quality network breaks into three components---a chain through Italy--Spain--Malta connected to a cycle with some Central and Eastern European countries (CEE), a separate cluster around Austria, Germany and the Netherlands, and Slovenia appearing as an isolated node (the reason it is dropped from the Quality peer-effect estimation in Section~\ref{sec:peer-effects}). Rather than a single EU-wide channel, Quality diffusion appears to operate through a small number of self-contained regional sub-systems. The Environment network is instead connected around a few high-in-degree hubs, notably Slovak Republic and Portugal, with its directed and entirely unreciprocated links producing a more hierarchical, star-like appearance.

Individual links admit plausible economic readings, and the Quality network illustrates two. Throughout, a directed link $i \rightarrow j$ means that $i$ influences $j$. In the first, Germany is recovered as the near-sole influencer of Austria ($\text{Germany} \rightarrow \text{Austria}$). The two economies form a tightly integrated manufacturing relationship---closely linked through the automotive sector in particular---and the link is consistent with diffusion along investment ties, a well-documented route for Quality \citep{corbett2006, arimura2011}. In the second---the chain running from Mediterranean economies to CEE---the recovered links tend to connect geographically and industrially proximate countries. I stress that such link-level readings are illustrative rather than causal. The systematic analysis of what predicts a recovered link follows in the dyadic regressions of Section~\ref{sec:dyadic}.

\subsection{Network Similarity}
\label{sec:network-similarity}
% =============================================================================
\input{assets/tables/jaccard_matrix.tex}

To move beyond visual inspection, I quantify the overlap between the two recovered networks through the Jaccard index, the share of links active in both networks among the links active in at least one; its formal definition and the full results are reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-similarity}. The index ranges in $[0,1]$, with $0$ for disjoint link sets and $1$ for identical ones.

The verdict is unambiguous. With $|E_Q| = 28$, $|E_E| = 30$ and only three shared directed links, the two recovered structures overlap at $J = 0.055$. This is essentially the overlap expected of two unrelated sparse networks on the same node set: were the two link sets placed independently at random over the $702$ ordered pairs, the expected number of shared links would be $|E_Q| \cdot |E_E| / 702 \approx 1.2$. The near-orthogonality anticipated visually in Figure~\ref{fig:networks} is therefore confirmed: the two standards do not diffuse over a common structure of influence.

Table~\ref{tab:jaccard_matrix} extends the comparison to the exogenous benchmarks of \citet{albuquerque2007} and delivers the same verdict for both standards: no proxy comes close to reproducing either recovered network.

\subsection{Dyadic Regression}
\label{sec:dyadic}
% =============================================================================

Section~\ref{sec:network-recovery} read individual links qualitatively and deferred to here the systematic question of what predicts a recovered link. I answer it with the dyadic regression of equation~\eqref{eq:dyadic}, projecting the recovered weights $\hat w_{ij}$ onto the six dyadic covariates, namely economic distance, environmental-policy asymmetry, cultural distance, geographic distance, bilateral FDI, and bilateral trade. Table~\ref{tab:dyadic_recovered_fp_fe} reports the estimates.

For Quality, bilateral FDI is the dyadic predictor of recovered link weights: a one-log-point increase in bilateral FDI raises the recovered weight by $0.0143$ (significant at $5\%$), so that a $10\%$ rise corresponds to roughly $0.0143 \times \log(1.10) \approx 0.0014$ of additional row-normalised weight: this represents a reallocation of country $i$'s recovered influence toward its investment partners. Bilateral trade, by contrast, is small and insignificant ($0.0031$). This refines the existing literature: \citet{guler2002} model the diffusion of the Quality standard through dyadic trade channels (trade cohesion and role equivalence), while FDI enters only as a country-level measure of a nation's overall inward investment rather than as a bilateral relationship; \citet{albuquerque2007} likewise build their economic channel from bilateral trade. Conditioning on both within the same specification, only FDI is significant.

For Environment, neither trade nor FDI is significant. The predictors that load instead are environmental-policy asymmetry ($0.0381$, significant at $5\%$) and economic distance ($-0.0360$, significant at $10\%$). Both enter as bilateral gaps in logged variables: a one-unit increase in the log gap of environmental tax per worker raises the recovered weight by $0.0381$, while a one-unit widening of the log GDP-per-capita gap lowers it by $0.0360$. Links thus form between countries with asymmetric regulatory postures but comparable economies, consistent with a governance-based rather than market-based logic \citep{berliner2013}. Neither cultural distance nor geographic distance is significant for either standard once sender and receiver effects are included, suggesting that the standard proximity proxies load primarily on country-level heterogeneity rather than on genuinely bilateral variation. The insignificance of geography in both specifications may reflect the homogeneity of the sample: the work that established its relevance \citep{albuquerque2007} drew on a broader, worldwide cross-section in which geographic distance varies far more than it does within the EU-27.

These findings complete the two-track reading: a technical standard whose network is predicted by economic integration, specifically FDI, and a political standard whose network is predicted by governance asymmetry.

\subsection{Peer Effects: Recovered vs Exogenous Networks}
\label{sec:peer-effects}

As anticipated in Sections~\ref{sec:methodology} and~\ref{sec:network-recovery}, I re-estimate the peer effect on a fixed $\hat W$ via the IV-2SLS second stage of \citet{bramoulle2009}. The cost of treating the recovered network as observed is the understating the uncertainty that the recovery stage carries. As validation of my recovered networks, I read the fit first. Figure~\ref{fig:bdf_r2_nolag} reports the within-$R^2$ of the IV-2SLS simultaneous specification for the recovered network and for each exogenous benchmark of \citet{albuquerque2007} namely: geography, culture, trade, geography + culture, geography + trade, where combined networks are obtained by considering links coming from both the single networks. The comparison is informative precisely because Section~\ref{sec:network-similarity} established that the recovered network is topologically unrelated to every benchmark. As expected, the recovered networks do show the best fit with respect to the exogenous benchmarks, and this also holds in the lagged specification (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:bdf_r2_fullag}), reinforcing that endogenous recovery adds information beyond the pre-specified channels. The validation is comparative, and a better fit need not rule out that the recovered structure partly absorbs synchronous pan-European shocks, a concern I return to in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. Figure~\ref{fig:bdf_forest_twfe_sim_vs_fullag} then reports the peer-effect estimates themselves, contemporaneous and lagged, across the same specifications.

For Quality, the recovered network yields a positive and significant peer effect of plausible magnitude ($\hat\rho = 0.40$) and attains the highest within-$R^2$ among all specifications ($0.109$ against at most $0.065$ for any exogenous proxy, Figure~\ref{fig:bdf_r2_nolag}). The exogenous proxies deliver estimates that are far larger (Geo~$+$~Trade at $1.13$, Trade at $1.91$), a pattern consistent with network misspecification loading common variation onto the peer term. The lagged specification reinforces this reading: the recovered network's lagged estimate ($0.08$) remains positive and marginally significant, whereas the exogenous estimates collapse toward zero or turn negative once the contemporaneous channel is shut down, suggesting that their large simultaneous coefficients were loading correlated shocks among structurally connected countries---which the year effects only partly remove---rather than genuine transmission.

For Environment, the recovered network delivers a small and insignificant simultaneous estimate ($\hat\rho = 0.16$), and no exogenous network yields a significant peer effect either. This symmetry locates the failure upstream of the network: since every specification---recovered and exogenous alike, including the \citet{albuquerque2007} benchmarks---identifies nothing, the limiting factor is not the choice of $\hat W$ but the data's ability to detect contemporaneous transmission at all. Two non-exclusive readings follow. The first is that the covariates available to me likely do not span the channel along which ISO~14001 actually diffuses: since the recovered quality network $\hat W_E$ is recovered from adoption covariation conditional on these covariates, a channel they fail to capture leaves $\hat W_E$ mismeasured, and the same covariate gap would leave any imposed $W$ unable to detect the true channel either. The second is that there may simply be little contemporaneous influence to detect in this period. The data cannot separate genuinely weak transmission from insufficient power, so the two readings compound rather than compete and neither lets me assert a true zero. \footnote{A purely second-stage consideration reinforces the imprecision without being an independent cause of it: given the fixed, hub-dominated $\hat W_E$, the indirect-peer instruments $W^2x$ and $W^3x$ carry limited independent variation, so the intransitivity condition of \citet{bramoulle2009} holds formally but yields weak instruments in practice. Because the recovered topology is itself an output of the recovery covariates, this is a symptom of the first reading above rather than a separate one.}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Mechanisms}
\label{sec:mechanisms}


The two-track contrast documented above admits a reading in terms of distinct diffusion mechanisms, consistent with the technical--political distinction drawn at the outset. For Quality, the main dyadic link predictor seems to be investment rather than trade, which is consistent with a supply-chain diffusion channel: the Quality standard is a private operational standard with no public-good rationale, and a well-documented route of its diffusion is the requirement, by large certified buyers, that upstream suppliers certify in turn \citep{corbett2006, arimura2011}. This channel operates at the firm level, whereas my FDI regressor is a country-level proxy for the durable buyer--supplier relationships along which such pressure travels; I therefore read the FDI result as consistent with, rather than direct evidence of, supply-chain transmission (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Such pressure is more likely to travel along the relationships that bilateral FDI marks, rather than along arm's-length trade flows.

For Environment, the dyadic results show that links arise between countries with asymmetric environmental-policy stances but comparable economies, a pattern consistent with adoption operating as a signalling device toward international audiences and more valuable where domestic governance is weaker \citep{berliner2013}. The dyadic regression results that identify as links' predictors economic distance and environmental policy asymmetry are consistent with what such a signalling logic would predict, with adoption oriented toward a small number of focal reference countries rather than exchanged reciprocally between peers. Whether adoption then propagates strongly along this structure is a separate question that the shorter Environment panel leaves unresolved: the recovered peer effect is small and imprecisely estimated.

I stress that these are interpretations consistent with the recovered structure and its dyadic correlates rather than causally identified mechanisms; the country-level data cannot separate coercion or signalling from correlated unobservables, and the readings above are offered in that spirit.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}


This work presents three main limitations worth discussing.

The first concerns the level of observation. Data are observed at the aggregate country level, so within-country heterogeneity---including the differential weight that large multinational firms may carry in transmitting standards through their supply chains---is subsumed into a single country-year observation. This is of particular concern for countries with dominant multinational sectors, where firm-level coercion is plausibly the primary channel. The country-level focus is nonetheless consistent with the policy diffusion literature, is the unit at which policymakers operate and is the only unit at which the ISO Survey data are publicly available. Moving to a firm-level network would multiply the parameter space by several orders of magnitude and render the sparse-recovery estimator infeasible at the available time-series length.

The second concerns the network-recovery methodology and data dimensions. The \citet{depaula2025} estimator is consistent as $T$ grows for fixed $N$; with $T = 25$ for Quality and $T = 21$ for Environment, both panels are short relative to the ideal, and the Environment panel is the more binding constraint. The weaker peer-effect identification and the wider GMM confidence interval for that standard are consistent with this. Extending the sample beyond 2017 would require a harmonisation of the pre- and post-2018 ISO Survey methodologies that was not supported by the current paper. Furthermore, the recovery methodology itself imposes necessary but restrictive structural assumptions. It requires the latent network to be fixed over the entire sample period; given that my panel spans over two decades, this static assumption precludes the possibility that channels of influence evolved as European integration deepened.

The third is the restriction of the sample to the 27 EU member states, which presents a specific tradeoff regarding unobserved confounders. On one hand, this homogeneity is an asset: it limits unobserved institutional heterogeneity and ensures that the regulatory baseline is highly comparable across nodes. On the other hand, it introduces the risk of unobserved common causes inducing spurious links. This risk takes two forms. First, internally: the EU is a highly active regulatory environment, and coordinated policy initiatives (such as European environmental directives or supply-chain mandates) may hit sub-clusters of member states at similar times, moving several countries together. While country and year fixed effects absorb uniform pan-European shocks, localised EU regulatory waves could induce synchronous adoption patterns that the recovery algorithm inadvertently attributes to peer-to-peer diffusion. Second, externally: because the node set is truncated at the EU border (Section~\ref{sec:data}), a non-EU country that influences two member states simultaneously---a shared trading partner, or a multinational headquartered outside the EU---leaves a trace of synchronous adoption between them that the estimator, seeing only the EU-27, may record as a direct link. In both cases the missing common cause, internal or external, is mechanically reattributed to a bilateral link.
\subsection{Extensions}
\label{sec:extensions}


Three extensions would deepen the analysis. First, the ISO Survey is public only at the national aggregate, but the organisation maintains a proprietary record of individual certificates by sector and site. Access to that dataset would allow the recovery exercise to be repeated within countries at the regional or sectoral level, testing whether the country-level networks recovered here aggregate consistently from finer-grained diffusion and addressing directly the first limitation above.

Second, the supply-chain mechanism formalised by the dyadic FDI result, could be tested where it actually operates: at the firm level, within documented vertical relationships. Matched buyer-supplier data in sectors with strong certification requirements, such as automotive, would permit a comparison of diffusion speed along supply chains against diffusion across unconnected firms, providing a micro-founded test of the coercive channel that country-level data can only infer.

Third, a harmonisation of the ISO Survey would open two further extensions. Extending the panel beyond 2017 requires reconciling the pre- and post-2018 survey methodologies, whose change in design is what motivates my 2017 cut-off in the first place; harmonising the two series would lengthen the time dimension and, in turn, allow the cross-section to be widened to include non-EU benchmarking countries. The additional temporal and cross-sectional variation would be valuable on two fronts. First, it would sharpen the network recovery itself, most of all for Environment. Second, it would help isolate peer-driven diffusion from broader, confounding EU institutional dynamics: because my significant findings remain closely tied to European bilateral investment channels and regional integration mechanisms, I cannot currently discard the hypothesis that the recovered networks are partially shaped by synchronised pan-European policy shocks or macroeconomic trends, and only a longer, wider panel would provide the leverage to separate genuine network diffusion from these localised dynamics.

\clearpage
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper asked whether a technical and a political standard diffuse through different international channels, and answered without fixing those channels in advance. Using annual Quality and Environment certification counts for the 27 EU member states, I recover a separate latent diffusion network for each standard with the adaptive elastic-net GMM estimator of \citet{depaula2025}. The two recovered networks turn out to be nearly disjoint, and neither resembles geography, trade, or cultural proximity. The dyadic regressions locate the difference: for Quality the binding bilateral margin is investment rather than trade, which places the ``economic channel'' of the prior literature in FDI rather than in trade; for Environment no economic variable predicts a link, which forms instead between countries with asymmetric environmental-policy stances but comparable economies, consistent with a governance-based logic. Holding the recovered network fixed, the second stage gives a positive and significant peer effect for Quality on the network that also attains the best fit, while for Environment the peer effect is small and imprecisely identified.

Ultimately, the recovered networks resemble none of the exogenous proxies, yet fit the adoption data better than all of them. The reach of these conclusions is bounded by the country-level data, the short panels, and the EU-27 sample. Within those bounds, the analysis establishes that a technical and a political standard form along distinct structures that no standard proximity metric captures, and leaves open how a finer-grained or longer panel would sharpen the channels recovered here, most of all for Environment, where the data identify which links form but not how strongly adoption travels along them.

\subsection{Network Similarity}
\label{sec:appendix-similarity}
This appendix gives the formal definition of the Jaccard index reported in Section~\ref{sec:network-similarity} and Table~\ref{tab:jaccard_matrix}. For two binarised directed link sets $A$ and $B$,
\begin{equation}
    J(A, B) = \frac{|E_A \cap E_B|}{|E_A \cup E_B|},
    \label{eq:jaccard}
\end{equation}
where $E_A = \{(i, j) : |w^A_{ij}| > 10^{-5}\}$ is the set of active directed links of network $A$, and $E_B$ is defined analogously. The index ranges in $[0, 1]$, equalling $0$ for disjoint link sets and $1$ for identical ones; it is computed on the support of each weight matrix, so it captures agreement in topology and is insensitive to the magnitude of the recovered weights. For the two recovered networks, $|E_Q| = 28$, $|E_E| = 30$, $|E_Q \cap E_E| = 3$ and $|E_Q \cup E_E| = 55$, giving $J(\hat W^Q, \hat W^E) = 0.055$.
